Food and blood sugar.

The same amount of carbohydrate can give different blood sugar curves. Here is why, and how you can find out what applies to you.

The food you eat can make your blood sugar rise in different ways. Here is a simple explanation.

Made for Dexcom sensor and Tandem pump. If you have another pump, this may be misleading for you.

Read this first

  • This is general information and not medical advice.
  • Do not use the times on this page to work out extra insulin on your own. With type 1 diabetes, both a delayed rise in blood sugar and active insulin can create a risk of high or low blood sugar. Follow the treatment plan from your diabetes team.
  • The CGM sensor can lag behind your blood sugar. If it does not match how you feel, the manufacturers (Dexcom, Abbott and Medtronic) recommend that you check with a fingerstick.

In short

  • Carbohydrate affects blood sugar the most. Fat, protein and fibre can make the rise later and longer.
  • How long and when blood sugar rises differs from person to person. There is no fixed “tail” for each food.
  • No food is forbidden. Some foods just need more planning, see the Watch-out list.
  • Your own sensor data says more about you than a general table.

Why the same food gives a different curve

Carbohydrate matters most for blood sugar, but it is not everything.

Carbohydrate
Gives the fastest and clearest rise. The amount matters most.
Fat
Slows down stomach emptying. The rise can come later and last longer.
Protein
Slows the absorption of carbohydrate. In some people with type 1 diabetes, large amounts can also cause a later rise.
Fibre
Slows absorption, especially in wholegrain products.

The glycaemic index (GI) tells you how fast a food raises blood sugar. Chocolate and crisps have a lower GI than you might expect, because the fat slows the absorption of the carbohydrates. A low GI still does not mean that the food gives a small effect. GI does not take portion size into account.

Other things can also change the curve: insulin and when it was taken, physical activity, stress, illness, sleep and what the food was eaten together with.

What is a “tail”?

A tail is when blood sugar keeps rising or stays high for several hours after you have eaten. This often happens when the food contains a lot of fat and protein together with carbohydrates.

Watch out! Foods that need extra planning

No food is forbidden with diabetes. Some foods just behave more unpredictably than others. It is wise to know which ones, and to talk to your diabetes team about how to handle them.

Watch out: can give a long tail

  • Snickers and chocolate: sugar gives an early rise, fat can give a later one.
  • Pizza: dough, cheese and meat can keep blood sugar up for several hours.
  • Burger and chips: carbohydrates and a lot of fat can give a delayed rise.
  • Cakes and biscuits: sugar and fat can give both an early and a late rise.

Watch out: often gives a fast rise

  • Glucose (dextrose) tablets
  • Sugary fizzy drinks and juice
  • Sweets with little fat
  • White bread and white rice

A faster rise, but not necessarily a long tail.

With a CGM sensor you can follow your blood sugar for several hours after the meal and see your own tail.

The sensor does not always show real time

A CGM sensor measures sugar in the fluid just under the skin, not directly in the blood. That is why the sensor value can lag a little behind, especially when blood sugar changes quickly, for example after a meal. The sensor and a fingerstick can also show different numbers for a while. If in doubt, do a fingerstick, and follow your diabetes team's advice on what to do with the result.

Why Snickers often stands out

Snickers has both sugar (early rise) and a lot of fat (later and longer rise). Studies in children and teenagers with type 1 diabetes have shown that fat can affect blood sugar for several hours. These are studies under controlled conditions, not a measurement of what Snickers does. In some people fat can also affect the insulin need. Talk to your diabetes team about how this is handled.

Glucose (dextrose) tablets

Glucose (druesukker) is pure sugar with no fat, protein or fibre to slow absorption. It is absorbed quickly into the blood, and blood sugar rises fast. How and when to use glucose, for example for low blood sugar, you agree with your diabetes team.

Make your own log

This is how you can find out how a specific food affects you:

  1. Write down what you ate and follow the curve for the next 4–6 hours. The list below shows what you can note.
  2. Compare several similar situations before drawing a conclusion. One measurement says little.

What you can write down

  • Date and time
  • Food and portion
  • Blood sugar before
  • Insulin and timing, as in the treatment plan
  • Activity before and after
  • Blood sugar after 2, 4 and 6 hours
  • Notes: stress, illness, sleep and anything else unusual

The template can be pasted into a spreadsheet or a notes app. Use the log to look for patterns, and bring it to your diabetes team. The site does not store anything you write.

Why does blood sugar go up differently?

When you eat, the food goes down into your stomach. Sugar from the food gets into your blood. We call that blood sugar.

But not all food does it in the same way.

Imagine a bonfire

Sugar is like paper on the fire. It catches fire fast and burns out fast.

Fat is like a thick log. It takes longer to light, but it burns for a long time.

Sometimes blood sugar stays up for a long time after the food. That is called a tail. Food with a lot of fat, like pizza and Snickers, can give a tail.

Choose a food

Glucose (druesukker) is pure sugar. It goes almost straight into the blood, and nothing slows it down the way fat does. That is why blood sugar goes up very fast. When you should take glucose is decided by an adult or the diabetes team.

The drawing is only a sketch to show the idea. Your own curve looks different.

Remember this

  • No food is forbidden. Some foods just need a little more planning by an adult or the diabetes team.
  • Different food gives a different curve.
  • Your curve is your own. It is not the same as other people's.
  • Always ask an adult or the diabetes team what to do.
  • The sensor is sometimes a little slow. If it does not feel right, tell an adult, and you can check with a fingerstick.

This page does not tell you what to take or do. If you feel unwell or strange, tell an adult right away. If it is very serious, you can call 113.
