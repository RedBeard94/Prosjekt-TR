How the numbers fit together.

The pump uses a few numbers from the diabetes team when it works out insulin. Here you can see what they mean, with an example that is nobody's own numbers.

Made for Dexcom sensor and Tandem pump. If you have another pump, this may be misleading for you.

Example numbers

Four numbers the pump uses

Every family has its own numbers, set by the diabetes team. The numbers below are only to show how it fits together.

Carbohydrate ratio1 : 10exampleOne unit of insulin covers 10 grams of carbohydrates.
Correction factor2mmol/L per unit, exampleOne unit of insulin lowers blood sugar by 2 mmol/L.
Target value6mmol/L, exampleThe value the pump aims for when it corrects.
Insulin duration4hours, exampleHow long the insulin works. The pump subtracts what is left.

What the numbers are called on MiniMed 780G

The pump uses other names than the examples on this page. Here is what is what. The pump menus are in Norwegian, so the Norwegian names are in brackets.

  • Carbohydrate ratio (Karbohydratforhold) (grams per unit). On this page: Carbohydrate ratio.
  • Insulin sensitivity factor (Insulinsensitivitetsfaktor) (mmol/L per unit). On this page: Correction factor.
  • Blood glucose target (BS-mål) (mmol/L). On this page: Target value.
  • Active insulin time (Aktivt insulin-tid) (hours). On this page: Insulin duration.
Active insulin is bolus insulin the pump has given that still lowers blood sugar. Insulin you took with a pen or syringe is not counted in the active insulin calculation.

Which numbers apply to you is decided by your diabetes team. Source: Medtronic's Norwegian user guide for MiniMed 780G (Bolus Wizard settings).

Example numbers

How the pump puts a dose together

A meal of 60 grams of carbohydrates, and blood sugar of 10 mmol/L.

  1. 1
    The food60 grams, and one unit covers 10 grams. That gives 6 units.
  2. 2
    Correct10 is 4 above the target of 6, and one unit lowers by 2. That gives 2 units.
  3. 3
    Put together6 and 2 make 8 units. If the child already has active insulin, the pump subtracts it.
You do not need to calculate this yourself. The pump does it when you enter carbohydrates and blood sugar. You only need to check that the numbers you enter are correct.

Where do the numbers come from?

The diabetes team finds the numbers by looking at how much insulin the child uses over a day and night and how blood sugar goes. Some also use two rules of thumb as a first estimate.

The 500 rule500 divided by the daily doseEstimates how many grams of carbohydrates one unit covers.
The 100 rule100 divided by the daily doseEstimates how many mmol/L one unit lowers blood sugar.
Example: A child uses 25 units a day. 500 divided by 25 is 20, so one unit covers about 20 grams of carbohydrates. 100 divided by 25 is 4, so one unit lowers about 4 mmol/L.
These are rough estimates, not a recipe. The 500 rule fits best when the basal insulin is set correctly. Studies of real-world settings show that many people use other numbers, and that children and teenagers deviate most. Children grow, and the need changes. The diabetes team adjusts the numbers along the way. Never use these rules to change settings yourself.

Sources: Sørlandet Hospital, procedure (in Norwegian) · DiabetesNet, 500 rule · Diabettech, the rules against real data
