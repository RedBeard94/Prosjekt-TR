It is urgent

Call 113 right away if the child
  • is limp, does not respond or is not fully awake

Also call the parents. Never give food or drink to a child who is not fully awake, and never give insulin during a hypo (low blood sugar).

  • Illness that cannot wait until daytime: the out-of-hours clinic (legevakt) 116 117.
  • Serious acute illness: 113.
  • Ask your diabetes team for a written plan for hypos and illness, and keep the team's number easy to find.

Fast sugar or glucagon?

Fast sugarGlucose tablets, juice, regular (not diet) soft drink or honey, when the child is awake and able to swallow.
GlucagonEmergency medicine that someone else gives when the child is unconscious or cannot swallow. Call 113.

Honey does not work like glucagon. It is fast sugar. Never give food or drink to a child who is not fully awake. Low blood sugar in children, step by step: Sørlandet Hospital (in Norwegian). First aid and your position for the emergency centre: the Hjelp 113 app.

Source: Diabetesforbundet: My kindergarten day with diabetes (in Norwegian) · Sørlandet Hospital: Low blood sugar in children (in Norwegian) · Norwegian Air Ambulance: Hjelp 113 (app)

Measuring blood sugar and the numbers

What is low and high?

Under 4Low. Take fast sugar.
4 to 10Good range
Over 10High

Numbers in mmol/L, according to Diabetesforbundet. Your diabetes team may set other targets for you or your child, and those apply first. Hospitals often give 4 to 7 as normal before a meal.

How to measure with a finger prick
  • Wash and dry your hands.
  • Put a new lancet in the lancing device.
  • Prick the side of your fingertip, and change finger each time.
  • Wipe away the first drop of blood.
  • Hold the test strip against the drop so the blood is drawn up.
  • Wait a few seconds. The number appears on the screen.
Unsure about the number from the sensor or pump? Take a control measurement with a finger prick, and go by the symptoms. More about why the numbers can differ: Food and blood sugar.

Source: Sørlandet Hospital: Measuring blood sugar (in Norwegian) · Dexcom: Sensor value and blood sugar value (in Norwegian) · Helse Bergen: Information about CGM (PDF, in Norwegian)

Carbohydrates and counting

  • Look at the package. The nutrition label gives carbohydrates per 100 grams. Work out what you actually eat.
  • Weighing the food now and then makes it easier to learn how much one meal is.
  • Food without a package: use the Plate. It takes the numbers from Matvaretabellen.
  • Learn from your diabetes team. They show how counting works for you, and which app they recommend.

When the child is ill

Children with diabetes do not get acutely ill more often than other children. But when they do get ill, you need to keep a closer eye on them.

  • Never stop the insulin, even if the child is not eating. The dose may need to go up or down, but you decide that together with the diabetes team.
  • Check more often than usual. The hospitals' advice is at least every 2 to 3 hours, also at night.
  • Check ketones in the urine when the child is ill, especially with vomiting or diarrhoea.
  • Make sure the child drinks enough. With vomiting, small amounts of a sugary drink, such as squash or a sports drink, can replace meals.
  • Call the hospital early. The threshold for getting in touch should be low, especially with vomiting and diarrhoea.
  • After illness the insulin need can be lower for one to two weeks. Keep checking often.
Doses and percentages are not given here. Ask your diabetes team for a written plan for illness, with who to call and when.

Source: Sørlandet Hospital: Acute illness and diabetes (in Norwegian) · NHI: Insulin treatment during infections (in Norwegian)

Travelling

Choose a section to open it.

Before you leave
  • Get an international diabetes ID card from Diabetesforbundet.
  • Check that your travel insurance covers diabetes. If not, you may need a separate policy.
  • If you are travelling in Europe: order a European Health Insurance Card from Helfo.
  • Teach the people you travel with to recognise low blood sugar, and how emergency medicine (glucagon) is used.
  • Talk to your doctor about what to do with vomiting, diarrhoea and fever, and whether you should bring something for diarrhoea.
What to bring
  • Enough insulin and equipment for the whole trip, plus spare.
  • Blood glucose meter and test strips. Extra battery for the pump.
  • An insulin pen as a backup, even if the child uses a pump.
  • Glucose tablets and other sugary food for hypos.
  • A cool bag or thermos for insulin. A thermometer if the insulin goes in a fridge.
Airport and plane
  • Keep insulin and all equipment in your hand luggage. The hold can get freezing cold, and luggage can be delayed.
  • Keep the medicine in its original packaging with the pharmacy label and name. Glucagon too.
  • Bring a doctor's letter in English. Some countries require a hospital stamp.
  • Pack the diabetes equipment in its own bag, so it is easy to take out at security.
  • Do not send the pump or sensor through X-ray. The manufacturers give different advice about body scanners and metal detectors. Check the manual, and ask for a manual check if you are unsure.
  • Contact the airline in advance about the pump and sensor, since they are wireless.
  • Take rapid-acting insulin only when the food is in front of you. Bring a packed meal and some quick sugary food.
  • It can help to use two clocks, one for home time and one for the time at your destination.
Time zones
  • North or south: doses are usually not adjusted.
  • East or west: when you cross several time zones, the doses may need to be adjusted. Agree a plan with your diabetes team before you leave.
  • With a pump: change the clock when you arrive. It is safer to have slightly higher blood sugar the first days than too low.
Heat, cold and storing insulin
  • Insulin cannot tolerate frost, direct sun or high temperatures. Do not leave it in the car.
  • At room temperature, up to 30 degrees, insulin usually keeps for 4 to 6 weeks. Check the package leaflet.
  • Hotel fridges can freeze insulin. Use a thermometer.
  • In the cold, the insulin pen can be carried close to the body.
  • The blood glucose meter works best between 15 and 35 degrees. Cold gives readings that are too low, and heat readings that are too high.
  • Heat and sun increase the absorption of insulin and give a greater risk of low blood sugar. Talk to the team about what to do in hot weather. Drink enough water.
Other countries and other units
  • Norway uses insulin with a strength of 100 IU/ml. Some countries use 80 or 40. The syringe must match the strength, otherwise the dose will be wrong.
  • Some countries give blood sugar in mg/dl. Divide the number by 18 to get mmol/L.

Source: NHI: Type 1 diabetes and travel (in Norwegian) · NHI: Air travel and type 1 diabetes (in Norwegian)

Teenagers, parties and alcohol

For teenagers and parents. Here are the facts, so it is easier to make good choices.

  • Alcohol lowers blood sugar, because the liver does not make sugar while it is breaking down alcohol. The risk is there for as long as there is alcohol in the blood.
  • Low blood sugar is harder to notice when you have been drinking.
  • A hypo can look like being drunk. Then help may come too late. That is why the people around you need to know what to look for.
  • Emergency medicine (glucagon) works less well after alcohol.

What helps

  • Check your blood sugar while you drink.
  • Eat food with carbohydrates before you go to bed, and while you drink.
  • Tell the people you are with what to look for.
  • Avoid home-distilled spirits and pure spirits. They give a high blood alcohol level in a short time.
  • Tell your parents or others at home that you have been drinking when you go to sleep.
  • Talk to your diabetes team about what applies to you, for example about insulin in the evening.

Source: Sørlandet Hospital: Alcohol and diabetes (in Norwegian)

Small children and kindergarten

Small children cannot always say how they feel. Then the adults have to watch how the child behaves.

  • Signs of low blood sugar can be paleness, hunger or thirst, difficulty concentrating and changed behaviour. The signs differ from child to child.
  • The child must always be allowed to eat when blood sugar is low. Keep hypo food at the kindergarten, for example juice, honey, raisins and glucose tablets.
  • Children with a pump should have an insulin pen available as a backup.
  • The child should have one fixed adult in charge. Agree who brings the equipment and hypo food on trips.
  • Wash your hands before checking. Dirty hands can give a wrong reading.
  • The parents train the adults and make a plan for hypos. The kindergarten sets aside time and informs all staff.
  • Give notice in advance about different days, such as trip days, carnival and end-of-term events.

Source: Diabetesforbundet: My kindergarten day with diabetes (in Norwegian)

About type 1 diabetes

  • The immune system attacks the cells that make insulin. The condition is lifelong, and you need insulin every day.
  • Around 30,000 people in Norway have type 1 diabetes. More than half are diagnosed before they are 18.
  • The condition often starts over a few days, with thirst, a lot of urinating, weight loss and feeling generally unwell.
  • A sensor measures sugar in the tissue about every five minutes.
  • Usual follow-up is a check-up every three to four months, and an annual check.

Source: NHI: Type 1 diabetes (in Norwegian)

Words and terms

Hypo (føling), low blood sugar
Blood sugar below 4 mmol/L.
High blood sugar
Above what is wanted. Diabetesforbundet says 4 to 10 mmol/L is a good range.
mmol/L and mg/dl
Two ways of giving blood sugar. Norway uses mmol/L. Divide mg/dl by 18.
Sensor, CGM
Measures sugar in the tissue around the clock.
Bolus
Insulin for a meal, or an extra dose to correct.
Basal
The steady insulin the pump gives around the clock.
Carbohydrate ratio and correction factor
Numbers from your diabetes team that the pump uses. See How the numbers fit together.
HbA1c
Long-term blood sugar. A blood test that shows how blood sugar has been over the past weeks.
Ketones
Substances the body makes when it lacks insulin. Can be measured in urine. Many ketones together with signs of illness is dangerous.
Glucagon
Emergency medicine that makes the body release sugar during a severe hypo.
IU
International units. The unit of measurement for insulin.
Suggestions

Ask the diabetes team

Questions that can be good to bring to the next appointment. The list is made by me, not by an official source.

  • What do we do if the child has vomiting or diarrhoea? Can we get a written plan?
  • Who do we call at night and at weekends?
  • Should we have emergency medicine (glucagon), and where should it be kept?
  • What do we do if the pump or sensor fails?
  • What do we do when travelling, and with time zones?
  • Can you help us make an action plan for the school or kindergarten?
  • What kind of follow-up and check-ups should we expect going forward?
